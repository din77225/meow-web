Camera and light

The vocabulary that makes a shot look chosen rather than generated. Keep this open while you write your shot list, and again while you write your prompts.

Why it mattersQuick guideShot typesMovementLightingGoing further

If you do not say how a shot is framed and lit, the model picks for you, and it picks the average: a medium shot, evenly lit, camera at eye level, subject in the middle. Do that ten times and you get ten shots that are individually fine and collectively lifeless, because nothing was chosen.

Everything on this page is film vocabulary, not AI vocabulary. It works on these models because they learned from film, and that is also why it transfers between tools.

One honest caveat. How well any single word lands depends on whether that model was trained on it, and every model reacts a little differently. A term that gives you a perfect low angle on one model may be interpreted loosely on another. Treat this page as a starting vocabulary, not a set of switches. If a word is not doing what you expect, describe the same idea in plainer language and see if that lands better.

If you are writing a shot list and do not know what to put Write it in plain words. "Close on her hands." "From behind him, wide." That is enough, and you can come back and use the proper terms once they mean something to you. Never leave the column blank just because you do not know the jargon.

The quick guide

If you read nothing else, read this. Which shot for which situation.

When you want toReach forWhy
Open, or re-establish where we areWideShows the place and the subject's relationship to it. Also the natural shot to end on.
Show what someone is doingMediumBody language plus enough context to read the action. Where most of a piece lives.
Show what something meansCloseEmotion, decision, reaction. Also the most reliable to generate, since less is in frame to go wrong.
Add a beat of rhythm cheaplyExtreme closeA hand, an object, a detail. Almost always generates cleanly and breaks up a run of similar shots.
Make someone feel powerfulLow angleCamera below eye level looking up.
Make someone feel small or watchedHigh angleCamera above looking down.
Put the viewer with them rather than watching themOver the shoulder, or from behindWe follow rather than observe. Also hides the face, which makes consistency far easier.
Build tensionSlow push inCamera moves toward the subject. Use sparingly, it is strong.
Reveal contextPull outCamera moves back to show more than you first understood.
Read the table as a habit, not a law These are what people normally reach for, not rules. Plenty of great shots break every line in it deliberately. Use it when you are unsure what to pick, and ignore it when you have a reason to.
The one rule underneath all of it Vary your shots. Ten shots at the same distance and angle feels flat no matter how good each one is. You do not need a system, you just need to not do the same thing every time.

Camera movement

Everything above is about where the camera is. This is about what it does, and it only exists in video. A still cannot show you a push in. These are the moves worth knowing, grouped the way they are actually used.

MoveWhat the camera doesWhat it does to the viewer
Push in
(dolly in)		Moves physically forward toward the subjectBuilds tension, draws you closer to a realisation. The most useful move in the list.
Pull out
(dolly out)		Moves physically backwardReveals context, or isolates someone by showing how much space is around them.
PanRotates left or right from a fixed pointFollows action, or reveals what was beside you. The camera looks; it does not travel.
TiltRotates up or down from a fixed pointReveals height or scale. Tilt up a building, tilt down to what someone dropped.
Track
(follow)		Travels with the subject, matching their pacePuts you alongside them rather than watching them pass.
Orbit
(arc)		Circles around the subject at a constant distanceBig, showy, and worth exactly one use per piece. Great for a reveal or a hero moment.
HandheldHuman-held, with natural sway and micro-correctionsImmediacy and unease. Documentary truth, or a scene that should feel unstable.
Crane / droneRises or descends through open spaceScale and geography. Opening and closing shots.
StaticNothing. Locked off.Lets the performance carry it. Not a failure to choose a move. Silence is a choice.
Push in versus zoom in, since people mix them up A push physically moves the camera closer, so the background shifts and the space feels real. A zoom changes the lens, so the background compresses and it feels observed rather than approached. If you want intimacy, push. If you want surveillance, zoom.
One rule worth keeping

One move per shot, on most models. Asking for a push in that becomes an orbit that rises into a crane is how a generation falls apart. The newest models can handle more than one move, but if yours is struggling, this is the first thing to simplify. Chain moves across cuts instead.

Lighting

Two decisions do most of the work: where the light comes from, and how hard it is. Move the light and the same face becomes a different character. Not an exhaustive list, and as with the shot types, different models respond to these words differently depending on what they were trained on. Fuller reference linked at the bottom.

LightWhat it doesGood for
SoftDiffused, gentle shadows, forgivingWarmth, calm, anything flattering
HardSharp shadows, high contrast, unforgivingDrama, tension, premium product work
Side lightSculpts the face, half in shadowConflict, character, seriousness
Top lightShadows in the eye socketsUnease. Use deliberately, it is unflattering
BacklightSubject separated from background, rim of lightSilhouette, mystery, hiding a face
RembrandtSmall triangle of light on the shadowed cheekClassic portraiture, quiet intensity
Tyndall / shaftsVisible beams through dust or mistAtmosphere, awe, a strong single source

The named setups worth knowing

You can get a long way with five words rather than a lighting diagram. Golden hour for warm, low, flattering sun. Blue hour for the cool minutes after sunset. Overcast for even, shadowless, honest. Single hard source for drama. Practical lights in frame, meaning lamps and signs that are visibly the source, for realism and warmth.

Naming one of those in a prompt is usually faster and more reliable than describing light physics.

The whole framework on one page

The last line is the one that catches people. "Soft diffused light" and "hard directional shadows" in the same prompt is a contradiction, and the model will pick one or average them into mud.
