Your concept and your shot list

A planning document, not a contract. Its job is to make you think before you act. You will change details all the way through as you learn what works, and that is expected. Start it in week one.

Why this exists The hardest part of making an AI video is not the technology, it is deciding what you are making. People who skip this step tend to start something new every week and finish nothing, because there is always a more exciting idea one prompt away. Fifteen minutes here is what makes the rest of it possible.

1What are you making?

Five short answers. Do not write a treatment and do not be clever. Deciding roughly now beats deciding perfectly in week four.

Name the format and the subject. If you cannot say it in one sentence, it is not decided yet.
This is your brief. It decides your aspect ratio, your length and your tone, so it is worth a real answer.
As short or as long as is useful to you. This is a way of thinking your piece through, not a document anyone marks. A few sentences is plenty for most pieces.

2Breaking it into shots

This is the part nobody teaches beginners, and it is the difference between a piece that gets finished and a folder of unrelated clips.

This is where you plan your scenes according to the story. Go through your piece and imagine how many seconds each moment needs on screen. A glance is two seconds. A slow walk down an aisle might be ten. A held final image could be eight.

Do not plan around what the AI can generate. That gets worked out later, and it should not shape what you write here.

More on writing a shot list, if you want it

Why not plan around the model. Generation lengths keep changing, and they vary a lot between models. If you plan around today's limits you write a worse piece and it dates quickly. Give each moment the time it needs, then in week three you work out how to actually produce it. Some shots you will generate one at a time; a run of consecutive shots can often be batched into a single multi-shot generation instead. That is a production decision and it comes later.

How many shots. However many the piece needs. As a rough sense of scale a 60 second piece often lands between six and twelve, but a slow held piece might be five and a fast montage twenty. Write the shots, add up the seconds, see where you are.

One continuous moment per row. Plenty can happen at once inside a shot: someone walks while rain falls while a car passes. What makes something a new shot is a cut, meaning a change of angle, place or time. So the question for each row is not "is this one idea," it is "does the camera cut here."

Vary your angles and distances. Ten shots from the same position at the same distance feels flat however good each one is. Move around: closer, further, higher, lower, from behind. The camera and light reference shows what each option looks like and when to use it.

Give it a beginning, a middle and an end. Even in thirty seconds. Something establishes where we are, something happens, something concludes.

A shortcut that works. Write the list as if describing the finished piece to a friend, one sentence at a time. "It opens on the empty street. Then the light comes on. Then closer, her hand on the switch." Those sentences already are your shots.

How close, and from where The camera and light reference has a card for every shot type with an example image, plus a quick guide for which shot suits which situation. Open it in another tab while you fill in the "how close" column. If you do not know the term for what you want, write it in plain words: "close on her hands", "from behind him, wide". That is a perfectly good answer.

3What your shot list says you need to build

Read down your own list and this writes itself. Anything appearing in more than one shot has to stay the same across all of them, which means it needs a reference image rather than a fresh description each time. That collection is your kit, and you build it in week two.

Go down your shot list and write down:

  • People who appear more than once. Each needs a character sheet. Someone in a single shot does not.
  • Places you return to. Each needs a location image. In the bookshop example that is three: the street, the shop interior, the doorway.
  • Objects that are in focus, or that a character handles. A product held up to camera, a vehicle someone drives, a prop that gets picked up. Those need their own reference. Anything sitting in the background does not: the model regenerates background objects plausibly every time and nobody watching is tracking them.
  • One look image. Everyone needs exactly one: the palette, the light and the mood of the whole piece in a single frame.

4Save it and send it

Nothing here is stored anywhere, so save it before you close the tab.

"Save as PDF" is inside your browser's print dialog, and prints a clean typed-up version rather than the form. "Send" opens your own mail app with everything written out, addressed to Mia and copied to you, so you can read it over before sending.

One last thing. This is a plan, not a contract. It will change, and that is normal. Its job is to make sure that when you sit down to generate, you already know what you are generating and why. And a note that applies from here to the very end: because generation depends partly on luck, a shot that is good enough is usually the right shot to keep. Holding out for perfect on any single shot is the most common way people run out of time and money before they run out of ideas.
